\section{Performance model}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Choice of model.} BFS executes almost no floating-point operations, so a classic
roofline in FLOP/byte~\cite{roofline} has nothing to place on its horizontal axis. The ECM
model needs a steady-state loop with predictable data transfers per iteration, so that the
in-core and transfer times can be added up. BFS has data-dependent early exits, atomic
updates and a work distribution that changes at every level, so ECM's assumptions do not
hold. We therefore use an information-exchange model in the spirit of roofline: we count
the bytes that must move and the random accesses that must be waited for, from measured
work rather than from the graph size. We bound the time by two machine limits, each
measured with our own benchmarks:
\begin{align*}
T_\text{bw}(p)  &= \frac{Q}{\min(p\,B_1,\;B_\text{all})}, &
T_\text{lat}(p) &= \frac{N_\text{rand}\; L_\text{DRAM}}{\text{MLP}\cdot p}, &
T_\text{model}(p) &= \max(T_\text{bw}, T_\text{lat}),
\end{align*}
where $Q$ is the data volume of one search, $B_1$ and $B_\text{all}$ the STREAM triad
bandwidth with one and all threads, $N_\text{rand}$ the number of random cache-line
accesses, $L_\text{DRAM}$ the dependent-load latency at \SI{1}{GB}, and MLP the number of
misses a core keeps in flight (Little's law). $T_\text{model}$ is a lower bound: it ignores
synchronisation and assumes every access goes to DRAM.

\paragraph{Parameters.} $Q$ and $N_\text{rand}$ follow from the edge counts of
Section~\ref{sec:app} (Table~\ref{tab:traffic}). In bottom-up, every checked vertex costs at
least one cache line of its neighbour list (its first neighbours usually suffice). In
top-down, every examined edge costs 4 bytes of neighbour ID plus one random line of
\texttt{parent}. Sweeps over \texttt{parent} (\SI{4}{B}), the CSR index (\SI{8}{B}) and the
bitmaps are sequential. MLP was measured with up to 32 interleaved pointer chases on one
core. On the laptop the time per load falls from \SI{262}{ns} with one chain to
\SI{17}{ns} with 16 chains, giving MLP $= 15.7$. This matches the 16 fill buffers of a Golden
Cove core. For Hábrók we currently assume MLP $= 10$ \todo{replace by measured value}.

Three regimes emerge:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single thread: latency-bound on both machines.} The latency bound predicts
        \SI{46.7}{ms} and \SI{19.2}{ms}; we measure 21\% and 30\% more. A single core cannot
        keep enough misses in flight to use the bandwidth: $T_\text{lat}$ is
        3--4$\times$ larger than $T_\text{bw}$.
  \item \textbf{Laptop, all threads: bandwidth-bound.} With 8 threads the latency bound drops
        below the bandwidth bound of \SI{11.3}{ms}. The measured \SI{8.4}{ms} is even
        \SI{26}{\percent} faster than this bound. Moving all \SI{179}{MB} from DRAM in
        \SI{8.4}{ms} would need \SI{21}{GB/s}, more than STREAM's \SI{15.9}{GB/s}, so part of
        the traffic is served from the \SI{24}{MB} L3: \texttt{parent} (\SI{4}{MB}), the
        bitmaps and part of the index fit there. The laptop is at its memory-bandwidth
        limit, which explains why more threads (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}) do not help.
  \item \textbf{Hábrók, many threads: neither bound.} Both bounds fall to about
        \SI{0.5}{ms}, but the best measurement is \SI{1.38}{ms} (2.5$\times$ the bound) and
        128 threads take 6.5$\times$ the bound. The missing time is synchronisation (about
        ten barriers per search across up to 128 threads), load imbalance, and cross-socket
        coherence traffic on shared data. These do not appear in a bandwidth/latency model.
        This is why the server is only 3$\times$ faster than the laptop. It also motivates
        optimisations that reduce the number of threads or the synchronisation per unit of
        work rather than the memory traffic.
\end{enumerate}
In terms of attainable performance ($m/T_\text{model}$): the DRAM-only bound of the laptop
corresponds to \SI{1.4}{GTEPS}, and with L3 reuse we already reach \SI{1.87}{GTEPS}, so
little can be gained there without reducing $Q$. On Hábrók the bound corresponds to
$\approx$\SI{28}{GTEPS} for \texttt{kron20}, against the \SI{11.4}{GTEPS} we achieve at best.
The server has headroom, but only for optimisations that address synchronisation and
placement, or that reduce $Q$ by improving locality.
