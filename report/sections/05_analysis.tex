\section{Performance analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Baseline.} On \texttt{kron20}, unmodified \gapbs{} with the default thread count
needs a median of \SI{8.58}{ms} per search on the laptop (IQR \SI{4.77}{ms},
\SI{1.83}{GTEPS}) and \SI{2.85}{ms} on Hábrók (IQR \SI{1.43}{ms}, \SI{5.51}{GTEPS}). The
server node has 12.8$\times$ more cores and 20$\times$ more bandwidth, yet it is only
3$\times$ faster. Explaining this gap is the main question of the analysis.

from perf's timer-based sampling (\num{46000} samples, 64 searches, all threads).

Half of all CPU time is threads waiting in the OpenMP runtime. A search consists of about ten
short parallel regions (Section~\ref{sec:app}), each ending in a barrier, and the work
inside them is irregular: Kronecker graphs have a heavy-tailed degree distribution, and in
bottom-up the cost per vertex depends on how soon a frontier neighbour is found. Among the
useful work, \texttt{BUStep} dominates by an order of magnitude. Instruction-level
annotation of \texttt{BUStep} (\texttt{pe/results/wsl/perf\_annotate\_all.txt}) puts
\SI{39}{\percent} of its samples on the instruction right after the load of the first
neighbour ID. Timer sampling skids to the instruction that consumes a long-latency load, so
this is the cache miss on the start of each neighbour list. A further \SI{11}{\percent} fall
on the bitmap bit-test, and about \SI{13}{\percent} on loads of the CSR index. The kernel is
dominated by memory accesses, not arithmetic.

\texttt{BUStep} takes \SI{78}{\percent} of the cycles even without any synchronisation, and
both steps run at an IPC around 1 on a core that can sustain several instructions per
cycle. In
\texttt{TDStep}, \SI{75}{\percent} of the examined edges miss, as expected for random
\texttt{parent[v]} accesses. In \texttt{BUStep} only a third of the checked vertices miss:
consecutive vertices' neighbour lists are adjacent in the CSR array, so the hardware
prefetcher hides many of them. The conversion kernels are streaming bit operations with high
IPC and negligible cost. The data TLB is not a factor on Hábrók: with transparent huge pages
always on, a search causes about 4,200 dTLB misses against roughly 2 million random line
accesses. Counter runs with 128 threads include the five slow warm-up trials, which dominate
their wall time, so we do not use them quantitatively.

\paragraph{Thread scaling.} Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows the time per search as a function
of the number of threads. On the laptop the best time is reached at 8 threads
(\SI{8.39}{ms}); going to 10 adds no speed and greatly increases variance (IQR
\SI{12.3}{ms}). On Hábrók, time decreases up to 48--64 threads bound to consecutive cores
(\SI{1.38}{ms}, \SI{11.4}{GTEPS}) and \emph{increases} again beyond, to \SI{3.53}{ms} at 128
threads. 64 threads bound with \texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=close} fill exactly one socket. Adding
the second socket adds cross-socket coherence traffic on \texttt{parent} and the bitmaps,
and more threads per barrier, while each step has less work per thread. Without binding,
the best time is \SI{2.45}{ms} at 48 threads.

\paragraph{Memory hierarchy.} Figure~\ref{fig:latency} shows the dependent-load latency of
both machines, measured with a pointer chase over a random cycle. The steps reveal the cache
sizes: on Hábrók latency stays at \SIrange{13}{15}{ns} up to \SI{32}{MB} (one CCD's L3) and
reaches about \SI{99}{ns} in DRAM. On the laptop, DRAM latency grows to \SI{376}{ns} at
\SI{1}{GB} because of TLB misses under nested paging. The working set of a \texttt{kron20}
search (Section~\ref{sec:model}) is about \SI{180}{MB}. It exceeds every cache of the laptop.
On Hábrók it exceeds a single CCD's L3 but fits in the 16 L3 slices combined, so much of it is
served from remote L3 slices at near-DRAM latency.
