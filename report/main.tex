\begin{abstract}
\noindent We study the breadth-first search (BFS) kernel of the GAP Benchmark Suite on two
systems: a laptop with a hybrid Intel Core i7-12650H (run under WSL2) and a dual-socket
AMD EPYC 7763 node of the Hábrók cluster. With perf, PAPI, gprof, a perf\_event-based
counter tool of our own and the benchmark's timers, all cross-validated, we identify the
bottom-up step and OpenMP synchronisation as the dominant costs. Instrumenting the kernel
shows that direction-optimising BFS examines only \SI{4.7}{\percent} of the edges of a
Kronecker graph with $2^{20}$ vertices. A traffic model built from these counts, STREAM
bandwidth and measured latency and memory-level parallelism predicts single-thread
performance within \SIrange{21}{30}{\percent}. It shows that the laptop is bandwidth-bound,
while the server node is limited by synchronisation and cross-socket effects. Of seven
optimisation strategies, three gave significant gains: degree-ordered relabelling (up to
$2.8\times$, also cutting the edges examined by up to \SI{48}{\percent}), choosing the thread
count and placement ($2.1\times$ on the server), and NUMA-aware first touch of the graph
($2.1\times$ with threads spread over both sockets). As the analysis predicts, compiler
flags, schedules, huge pages and prefetching stayed within run-to-run noise.
\end{abstract}
